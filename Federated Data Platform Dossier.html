Your seat

Your group now runs the FDP

It is Wednesday 7 October 2026. On Monday a new senior responsible owner (SRO) took charge of the NHS Federated Data Platform (FDP), the £330mn system Palantir built to join up hospital data in England. Your group is the SRO's team.

The previous SRO left a file. It describes one problem: resistance. Analysts refuse to log in. Campaigners file freedom of information requests. MPs want the contract ended. The file recommends a stronger adoption drive and better communications.

The health secretary wants something from your team by Friday. In March 2027 the government can break Palantir's contract. Downing Street is looking for a British alternative. Before ministers decide, he wants to know what people are actually objecting to, and what would change their minds.

The file may be right. Your group's job is to find out.

Summary

Listening to objections

Before you respond to an objection, work out which question it is asking.

When you hear…Questions to askWhat to do

'It doesn't benefit me'

'It doesn't do anything new for us.' 'We already have better tools.' 'Show me it gets there faster.'
  • What will this cost you in time or work?
  • What do you have today that works?
  • What would you need to see before you switch?
  • Did you have any say in this?

Change something real: the deal, the workload, the design, or who decides. Show a local benefit, in their terms, from people doing the same job. Let them keep what already works.

Avoid answering with national figures, such as 110,000 extra operations. Avoid a training drive that implicitly says 'you are not seeing any benefits because you are using it wrong'.

'It isn't fair'

'I refuse to work on this.' 'It was stitched up.' 'Who can see our patients' data?'
  • What exactly is wrong: the outcome, the way it was decided, who decided, or who we are working with?
  • What would a fair process look like to you?
  • What did we promise, and have we kept it?

Give people a real say, and change the process or the governance where it is wrong. Tell people before others find out. Keep your promises, or reopen them in public.

Avoid softening critics, moving objectors out of sight, or making changes behind their backs.

'It doesn't make sense'

'A database with some applications on top.' 'It doesn't do what it says on the tin.' 'How does that compare with hospitals not using it?'
  • Which part doesn't add up?
  • What did you expect it to do?
  • What evidence would convince you?

Explain plainly what it does and what it doesn't do. Show it working, including where it doesn't. Put the caveats where people will read them.

Avoid a bigger communications campaign, or hiding the caveat on a page few people read.

More than one at once

'It's a mix of not needing to, because other methods are better, and ethics.'
  • Which part matters most to you?
  • If we fixed that, would you still object?

Answer each part separately. A fix for one will not settle the other.

Avoid labelling people as resisters.
Further reading · The story

From a £1 pandemic deal to a break clause

In the spring of 2026 an NHS data official described what it was like to use the Federated Data Platform: 'It makes me feel sick every time I log into the thing.' The official was not alone. A growing number of NHS analysts were refusing to work on the platform, which Palantir had built under a £330mn contract to join up hospital data in England.

By then the FDP was the most contested technology contract in British government. Ministers had sought advice on a break clause. Within two months a Commons committee would ask the government to end the contract.

A pound in a pandemic

Palantir entered the NHS in March 2020 for £1. Its Foundry software tracked ventilators and protective equipment, and later vaccine logistics. In December 2020 a £23.5mn two-year contract followed, to help cut elective waiting lists. It put Palantir engineers inside hospitals. Chelsea and Westminster Hospital NHS Foundation Trust piloted Foundry in 2021. Matthew Swindells sat on Palantir's health advisory board from September 2019 until April 2022, when he became the trust's joint chair.

Officials told the FT that Palantir excelled at cultivating senior relationships. They described Ming Tang, then NHS England's data chief, as one of its chief evangelists inside the health service.

The tender, 2023

NHS England put the national platform out to tender in January 2023. It called the process 'rigorous, competitive, fair and transparent', run over 11 months. Inside the building, officials said, 'Foundry' and 'FDP' were already used interchangeably. The tender documents carried screenshots of Palantir's system with the logo obscured. At least two UK bidders withdrew. The final round had three: a Palantir-led consortium with PwC, Accenture and two UK healthcare consultancies; Oracle Cerner; and IBM with the British firm Quantexa.

NHS England chose Palantir. The Cabinet Office pushed to split the contract so the NHS would not depend on one supplier. Only one piece came out, a £28mn privacy-technology contract for Iqvia. Under the pricing, one person close to the talks said, it cost more to deploy the system to fewer hospitals than to many. One official said the Cabinet Office's concerns were 'overrun by a pincer move between NHS England and Number 10'.

In August 2023 the national data guardian, Nicola Byrne, prepared a statement on public concerns about 'privacy, procurement and commercial matters'. She shared an advance copy. Tang wrote back that it would 'create significant problems for me and the programme' and asked for softer language. Byrne delayed publication for 'a substantial rewrite'. The public learnt of the exchange three years later.

In November 2023 Palantir won a seven-year contract worth £330mn. A ministerial statement told Parliament that 'no new data will be collected, and GP data will not be part of the national platform'. A break clause falls in March 2027.

Rollout, 2024–2025

On paper the programme succeeded. The government's projects authority rated it green. By the end of February 2026, 123 of 205 hospital trusts used the platform and 80 reported benefits. Trusts could choose from up to ten tools. The theatre-scheduling tool ran in 39 trusts, the discharge tool in 25, the waiting-list tool in 23 and the cancer tool in two.

At University Hospitals of Northamptonshire, staff said weekly spreadsheet updates had gone and more than 1,000 patients had moved between hospitals to be seen sooner. Elsewhere, data teams said they already had richer tools. Greater Manchester did not sign up. Officials privately conceded that only a fraction of signed-up trusts used the platform fully.

In June 2025 officials briefed the health secretary, Wes Streeting, that public perception of the FDP 'has been affected by the profile of Palantir'. They suggested considering 'ways in which we can mitigate this'.

Spring 2026

Palantir's software served US Immigration and Customs Enforcement and the Israel Defense Forces. Its chief executive, Alex Karp, backed Donald Trump's immigration crackdown. In March the FT reported that Swindells had urged NHS colleagues in May 2024 to flow GP patient data into the FDP while he was advising Palantir through the lobbying firm Global Counsel. He said none of it had been actioned and that he had followed the NHS's governance rules.

On 2 April the FT reported that a growing number of NHS staff refused to work on the FDP. Managers treated the refusals as a 'workplace adjustment' and found them other projects. Streeting said he understood the concerns: 'there's a question people have about values.' In April Trump praised Palantir's 'great war fighting capabilities'. NHS staff blockaded the entrance to NHS England's headquarters.

In May the FT reported that NHS England had created an admin role giving a small number of external staff, Palantir's among them, 'unlimited access' to identifiable patient data in one part of the platform. The original data protection impact assessment had not disclosed it. In June a health minister apologised. The same month, London's mayor blocked the Metropolitan Police from awarding Palantir a contract worth up to £50mn.

Summer 2026

NHS England and Palantir defended the platform on results: 110,000 additional operations, a 15 per cent fall in discharge delays, 800,000 patients removed from waiting lists. Louis Mosley, head of Palantir UK, said: 'I want us to be judged on the merits of the programme. So, actually, I welcome the scrutiny.'

On 3 June the Commons science, innovation and technology committee asked ministers to use the 2027 break clause. On 16 June an FT analysis of data released under freedom of information showed that one trust, Chelsea and Westminster, accounted for 84 per cent of the 217,846 outpatient waiting-list removals cited. Thirteen of the 41 trusts using the theatre tool performed fewer procedures after adoption. Nine days later the FT found that NHS England had added a caveat to its methods page, seven times over: it could not 'draw conclusions about cause and effect'. The Office for Statistics Regulation opened an investigation.

In July the Health Foundation reported 'no noticeable improvement' in discharge performance at trusts using the discharge tool, and no meaningful difference from trusts without it. NHS England had held that analysis since December 2025. Its own internal rerun on long-stay patients suggested a fall of about 5 per cent, a third of the headline. Emails showed that an NHS England analyst had written of the operations baseline: 'I don't think this is a valid baseline.' Imperial College London's independent evaluation will report in 2029, two years after the break clause.

In August the government's annual projects report raised the FDP's lifetime cost to £1.1bn and cut forecast benefits to £808mn. The programme kept its green rating. NHS England called the higher cost 'a clear sign of confidence in the programme'. The FT also identified at least six senior health leaders with declared financial or professional links to Palantir, all within the rules.

Autumn 2026

In September the FT reported that NHS Cheshire and Merseyside had added data from five GP practices to its local version of the FDP, under local agreements. Officials said the 2023 promise covered only the national platform. The same month the Byrne emails surfaced. Her office said it had kept editorial control, and added the line that gives this case its title.

In October the FT found that more than half of the 40 trusts behind NHS England's waiting-list claim no longer used the tools. More than 60 per cent of the 31 trusts behind a separate 1.2mn-removals claim said they could not quantify the benefit. The statistics regulator opened a second investigation. Andy Burnham, now prime minister, wants government spending to go to British firms. Ministers are weighing a 12-month extension, a split contract and a British replacement; Quantexa is among three or four candidates. Tom Bartlett, who led the NHS England team that built the FDP, called replacement 'a leap into the unknown'.

The break clause falls in March 2027. Ministers have not yet decided.
